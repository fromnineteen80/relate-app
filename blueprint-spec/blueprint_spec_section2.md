# RELATE DATING BLUEPRINT
## Technical and Clinical Specification
### Section 2: The Four Quadrants

---

## Architecture Overview

The Blueprint measures four distinct dimensions of relational psychology. Each dimension is called a Quadrant. Within each Quadrant, the user lands in a named position called a Profile. The scoring inputs that produce the Profile are called Dimensions. A fourth term, Axis, is used internally within the technical architecture and never surfaces to the user.

The four Quadrants are not parallel structures. They operate at different levels of the person and measure different kinds of things. Quadrant One operates at the level of history. Quadrant Two operates at the level of emotion. Quadrant Three operates at the level of cognition and behavior under load. Quadrant Four operates at the level of identity. Together they produce a complete portrait of how this person loves, organized from the deepest layer outward.

The Quadrants interact with each other. They are not independent measurements that average into a composite score. Specific combinations across Quadrants produce emergent patterns that no individual Quadrant could identify alone. The cross-Quadrant interaction logic is documented in Section 5 of this specification. Every section of the generated report draws on multiple Quadrant results, and the synthesis section that closes the individual report is built entirely from cross-Quadrant inference.

---

## QUADRANT ONE: RELATIONAL HISTORY

### What It Measures

The formative wound architecture. Not what happened to the person, but what their nervous system concluded about love as a result of what happened, and whether that conclusion was ever revised. Two people with identical attachment types can have completely different relational histories that explain why they express that attachment type the way they do, and why the same relational intervention works for one and fails completely for the other.

### The Core Diagnostic Question

What did love teach you, when did it teach you that, and did anything ever come along to correct the lesson?

### The Three Scoring Dimensions

**Dimension A: Disruption Character**

Chronic and ambient versus acute and event-based.

Chronic disruption is atmospheric. It is the household where affection was conditional on performance, where a parent was physically present but emotionally somewhere else, where conflict was avoided so completely that the child never learned it could resolve, where love was communicated through provision or criticism or silence rather than through warmth. These people often cannot name what went wrong. There was no incident. There was a feeling, sustained over years, that shaped the nervous system before language was available to describe it. They frequently describe their childhood as fine. The wound is not in what happened but in what was consistently absent.

Acute disruption has an address. A parent who left. A death that was not grieved properly. A betrayal. A rupture that was never repaired and never explained. The nervous system organizes around preventing recurrence of that specific event. These people can often point to a before and after. They know something changed. The clarity is real and also partial. Knowing the origin of a wound does not automatically mean understanding how it operates in the present.

**Dimension B: Source Figure**

Caregiver origin versus romantic origin.

Caregiver-origin wounds are older, more embedded, and more likely to feel like personality rather than history. They are ego syntonic. The person experiences the pattern as just how they are rather than as something shaped by specific relational conditions. They often require more prompting to surface because the person has no memory of being different. The wound predates the self that would notice it.

Romantic-origin wounds are more accessible to conscious reflection. The person can usually name the relationship, describe what happened, and identify the pattern. The accessibility is useful and also misleading, because the wound feels like it belongs to that specific person rather than to a pattern the person carries into every relationship. These people often describe themselves as not always like this. There is a before-version they remember and an after-version they are now living. The therapeutic task is helping them see that the romantic wound activated something that was already there rather than creating it from nothing.

**Dimension C: Repair History**

Repaired versus unresolved.

This is the dimension most prognostically significant of the three, and the one most attachment frameworks omit entirely. A significant rupture followed by genuine repair changes the formative impact of the disruption substantially. Repair means a parent who came back and acknowledged what happened, a relationship that ended with real closure, a period of genuine therapeutic work that processed the wound rather than simply described it. The nervous system learns that rupture is survivable and that repair is possible. This does not erase the wound but it changes what the wound concludes. Repaired disruption produces people who carry fear but also carry evidence against it.

Unresolved disruption remains metabolically active. The nervous system is still working on it. These people are not defined by weakness or damage. They are defined by incompletion. Something did not finish. The grief was not grieved, the betrayal was not processed, the parent never acknowledged what they did, the relationship ended without explanation. The unfinished thing pulls at present relationships in ways the person can often feel but not locate.

Partial repair is scored as a distinct position rather than forced into either pole. Something was acknowledged but not fully processed, or the person did significant work but the wound site remains tender under specific conditions. This is its own clinically meaningful position and the report language for it differs from both the repaired and unresolved positions.

### The Six Profiles

The three Dimensions combine to produce eight theoretical positions that cluster in practice into six meaningful Profiles. Two theoretical combinations are rare enough to collapse into adjacent Profiles.

**Profile One: Chronic, Caregiver, Unresolved**

The wound is structural and invisible. These people often have the most difficulty recognizing themselves as shaped by their history because the history feels like neutral ground. They did not learn that love is dangerous. They learned that love is just like this, requiring management, contingent on behavior, better held at a slight distance. The unresolved quality means the nervous system is still operating on the original conclusions without any corrective data. They are often high-functioning, self-aware in intellectual terms, and genuinely surprised when partners identify patterns they cannot see in themselves.

**Profile Two: Chronic, Caregiver, Repaired**

The formative conditions were difficult but something came along, a relationship, a therapeutic process, a period of genuine self-reckoning, that provided corrective experience. These people have a quality of hard-won groundedness. They understand their patterns with real depth because they have had to work for that understanding. The chronic origin means the work is ongoing rather than complete, but the repair means they carry evidence that the original conclusions were not the whole truth.

**Profile Three: Chronic, Romantic, Unresolved**

The person had a functional early template that was eroded slowly by one or more long relationships. They often describe themselves as having been more open, more trusting, more willing. The chronic nature of the romantic wound means they often cannot identify a specific moment of injury, only a gradual accumulation of evidence that changed what they were willing to risk. The unresolved quality means they are still inside the story of that relationship in ways that shape how they read current partners.

**Profile Four: Chronic, Romantic, Repaired**

Similar erosion history but with genuine processing. These people often have the clearest self-knowledge of any Profile because the wound was accessible enough to examine and they did the work. They can describe their patterns with real precision. The risk is that the self-knowledge becomes a substitute for vulnerability. Knowing yourself very well can be a sophisticated way of remaining at a safe distance from the actual experience of being known by someone else.

**Profile Five: Acute, Caregiver, Unresolved**

There is a specific rupture, it came from a primary attachment figure, and it was never resolved. This is the Profile with the most active nervous system load. The specificity of the wound combined with its source combined with its incompletion produces a person who is often both highly attuned to relational threat and highly resourceful in managing it. They have had to develop considerable capacity to function despite the unresolved load. That capacity is real. It is also costly.

**Profile Six: Acute, Romantic, Unresolved**

Betrayal, sudden loss, an ending without explanation. Recent enough or unprocessed enough to still feel like evidence rather than history. These people are often the most consciously aware of their patterns and simultaneously least able to interrupt them because the wound has not yet separated from the present enough to examine. They frequently enter new relationships with explicit awareness of what they are doing and find that awareness does not protect them from doing it anyway. This is not a failure of intelligence. It is what unresolved acute wounds do. They operate faster than reflection.

### Scoring Note

The questions for this Quadrant work indirectly. No one answers a question about whether their childhood disruption was chronic or acute with diagnostic accuracy from direct inquiry. The questions ask about emotional climate, about specific memories, about what happened to conflict in the household, about whether the person remembers a specific moment or only a feeling, about what their earliest models of love looked like, about the relationship that shaped them most and how it ended. The answers to those questions allow inference of the three Dimensions. The person does not need to know what is being scored.

## QUADRANT TWO: TRIGGER EMOTION

### What It Measures

The primary emotional driver underneath attachment behavior. Not the emotion the person expresses. The emotion that is running the show when attachment anxiety activates, the one that precedes all the behavior, most of which is defensive maneuver organized around it.

Two people with identical attachment types and identical relational histories can behave completely differently in conflict and require completely different relational interventions because their trigger emotion differs. This is the layer that explains why attachment type descriptions feel incomplete to people who know their type well. The type describes what you do. The trigger emotion explains what you are doing it for.

### The Core Diagnostic Question

When the relationship feels threatened, what is the emotion underneath the response, before the defense mechanism activates?

### The Five Trigger Emotion Profiles

These are not personality traits. They are the specific emotional frequency the nervous system defaults to under relational threat. Each produces a recognizable behavioral signature that looks similar on the surface to the signatures produced by other trigger emotions but operates through completely different internal logic.

**Profile One: Fear of Abandonment**

The nervous system is organized around preventing disappearance. The threat is not conflict, criticism, or distance. It is gone. These people can tolerate almost anything except absence. Silence reads as ending. Slow replies read as withdrawal. Distance reads as the beginning of loss. Their behavior under threat is pursuit, escalation, testing, or preemptive self-abandonment to prevent being left first. The irony is that the pursuit behavior often produces the distance that confirms the fear. The nervous system generates the evidence for its own conclusion.

**Profile Two: Shame**

The nervous system is organized around concealment. The threat is exposure, being truly seen and found inadequate, defective, or too much. These people do not fear abandonment exactly. They fear that if someone stays long enough to really know them, they will eventually leave for a reason that confirms what the person already suspects about themselves. Intimacy increases threat rather than decreasing it. Their behavior under threat is withdrawal, deflection, preemptive self-criticism, or performance of competence and self-sufficiency. They often appear avoidant but the mechanism is different. They want connection. They are protecting against the verdict.

**Profile Three: Contempt**

The nervous system uses superiority as a buffer against vulnerability. The threat is dependency, needing someone who may not be adequate to be needed by. These people often hold high standards that function as a protection system. When the relationship feels threatening, they locate the problem in the partner's inadequacy rather than in their own fear. They are not indifferent. They are managing something underneath the contempt that they cannot easily access. The contempt is what happens when someone who needs connection is not able to tolerate needing it.

**Profile Four: Grief**

The nervous system is oriented around loss that has not fully resolved. These people are not afraid of what might happen. They are still metabolizing what already did. They bring a quality of mourning into relationships, not depression exactly, but a kind of pre-nostalgia, an anticipatory sadness. They love people with awareness of losing them. This produces a tenderness that partners often find profound and eventually exhausting. Their behavior under threat is not panic or rage but a slow, quiet withdrawal that can look like peace and is actually resignation.

**Profile Five: Rage**

The nervous system uses anger as a primary protective mechanism. The threat is powerlessness, being at the mercy of another person's choices. Rage is not aggression here. It is a sovereignty response. These people often carry a deeply held internal rule about fairness, reciprocity, or respect that when violated produces an intensity that surprises even them. The anger is real. Underneath it is usually fear or grief that the nervous system has learned is not safe to express directly. Rage is faster and feels less exposed.

### Scoring Note

The trigger emotion is scored independently and carries into the report as a modifier on attachment type. An Anchored attachment type with a shame trigger reads and behaves completely differently from an Anchored type with a rage trigger. The report section for this Quadrant makes that specificity explicit. The questions do not ask what emotion the person feels in conflict. They ask about specific past moments, about what happens in the body before the behavior starts, about what the person is most afraid of losing when a relationship feels threatened. The scoring infers the trigger emotion from the pattern of responses rather than from direct self-report.

## QUADRANT THREE: DECISION ARCHITECTURE

### What It Measures

How the person processes relational ambiguity and makes choices under emotional load. Not how they make decisions generally. Specifically how they operate when the relationship is unclear, threatening, or requires them to act without enough information to act rationally.

This is the most consequential and least examined territory in relational behavior. The moment between feeling something and doing something. What happens in that gap determines almost everything about how relationships develop, stall, rupture, and repair. Two people with identical attachment types and identical trigger emotions will still have completely different relational trajectories if their decision architecture differs.

### The Core Diagnostic Question

When relational ambiguity activates emotional load, what cognitive and behavioral strategy does the person default to before they have enough information to act from genuine clarity?

### The Six Decision Mode Profiles

**Profile One: Intellectualization**

The person moves threat from the emotional register into the analytical register as quickly as possible. They research, theorize, categorize, and explain. They are often the person who has read every attachment book, can describe their patterns with clinical precision, and still repeats them. The understanding is genuine. It just operates in a different system from the one running the behavior. Under relational threat they generate frameworks, timelines, probability assessments, and comparisons to past relationships. The analysis feels like control. It is actually distance from the emotional signal that would tell them what to do.

**Profile Two: Impulsive Action**

The person eliminates ambiguity by forcing a resolution. They send the text, make the call, issue the ultimatum, end the relationship, or declare their feelings, not because the moment calls for it but because the uncertainty is more intolerable than the potential consequences of acting. The action is often regretted not because the underlying need was wrong but because the timing and form were driven by anxiety rather than clarity. These people are frequently described by partners as intense, reactive, or hard to read because the action does not always match the apparent stakes of the situation.

**Profile Three: Consensus Seeking**

The person cannot act without external validation of their perception. They call friends, consult people who know the partner, and replay conversations with others to check their read. This is not indecisiveness. It is a specific deficit in trust of their own emotional signal. At some point they learned that their internal read was unreliable or unsafe to act on. The consensus seeking is an attempt to borrow certainty from outside because generating it internally feels dangerous. The problem is that nobody else has access to the actual data, and the advice they receive is therefore structurally unable to resolve the ambiguity.

**Profile Four: Silence and Withdrawal**

The person goes quiet. Not as punishment, not as strategy, but as the only available regulation response. They need to get out of the field of the relationship in order to think. They cannot access their own position while in proximity to the other person's emotional state. The withdrawal reads to partners as stonewalling, disengagement, or punishment. It is usually none of those things. It is a person trying to find themselves in a moment when the relationship has temporarily dissolved the boundary between their internal state and their partner's. The silence rarely comes with explanation, so the partner fills the gap with their own fear.

**Profile Five: Catastrophic Forward Projection**

The person's mind moves immediately to the worst resolved endpoint. Not anxiety about what is happening now but a fully rendered version of how this ends badly. They are not irrational. The projections are usually internally consistent and often based on accurate pattern recognition from past relationships. The problem is that the projection becomes the operating reality before any evidence justifies it. They are grieving or defending against an outcome that has not happened. Decisions get made in relation to that imagined future rather than the actual present situation. Partners experience this as a kind of premature verdict.

**Profile Six: Dissociative Backward Anchoring**

The person's mind moves to a past reference point rather than forward. When the current relationship becomes threatening or ambiguous, they locate themselves in a previous relationship, either a good one that this one is failing to match or a bad one that this one is beginning to resemble. The present moment loses resolution. They are partly somewhere else. Decisions get made with reference to that past template in ways the current partner cannot see or account for. These people often describe current partners in terms that clearly belong to someone else without being aware they are doing it.

### Scoring Structure

Unlike the previous Quadrants which produce a single named Profile, Decision Architecture scores the person on two internal axes that together determine which Profile is dominant and how rigidly it operates.

The first axis measures direction. Does the default mode move toward the threat (intellectualization, impulsive action, catastrophic projection) or away from it (consensus seeking, silence, backward anchoring)?

The second axis measures register. Does the default mode operate primarily in the cognitive register, the behavioral register, or the relational register?

The combination of direction and register produces a modifier that shapes specific sections of the report, particularly the communication section, the conflict section, and the decision-making section. These axes are internal architecture and do not surface to the user. The user sees their named Profile. The axes determine how the report speaks to that Profile.

### Scoring Note

Nobody decides to send the 2am text. Nobody decides to go silent for three days. These are not decisions. They are the decision architecture running without supervision. The questions for this Quadrant ask about specific past moments of relational ambiguity, about what the person did in the gap between feeling something and acting, about what they told themselves in that gap, and about what happened as a result. The pattern across those specific moments produces the Profile. The person does not identify their own Profile. The scoring derives it.

## QUADRANT FOUR: PERSONA IN PRACTICE

### What It Measures

How the person is currently living their RELATE persona in dating contexts specifically. Whether the persona is being expressed as genuine character or deployed as a defense system. How consciously they relate to it. And where romantic activation amplifies the persona beyond its healthy range into a version that costs the relationship.

This Quadrant does not re-measure the persona. The RELATE assessment has already established who the person is across four dimensions. Quadrant Four takes that as a fixed coordinate and goes somewhere the assessment cannot go from the outside. It asks what it is like to be this person inside a relationship. Where the persona serves them. Where it costs them. And where it has quietly become the primary mechanism for avoiding something they have not yet named.

The distinction between a person expressing their persona and a person whose persona is running them matters more than almost any other variable in relational psychology. The same persona at different levels of rigidity, awareness, and romantic amplification produces completely different relational outcomes. Two people who both scored Leadership in the RELATE assessment will have very different experiences of intimacy, conflict, and vulnerability depending on whether Leadership is something they do or something that is doing them.

### The Three Universal Axes

These axes apply across all 32 RELATE personas. They are the fixed scoring layer that makes Quadrant Four dynamic rather than a static lookup table. The user sees their results through the lens of their specific persona. The axes are the instrument that makes those results individualized.

**Axis One: Expression versus Defense**

Every persona has an authentic register and a defensive register. In the authentic register, the persona is a genuine expression of how the person is built, their natural orientation toward the world, other people, and intimacy. In the defensive register, the same persona has become organized around avoiding a specific kind of exposure. The behavior looks identical from the outside. The internal architecture is completely different.

The RELATE persona metadata already contains the shadow. The struggles and disappointments fields for each persona describe exactly what happens when the persona tips from expression into defense. The Presence persona in its authentic register makes partners feel genuinely seen. In its defensive register it becomes a strategy for remaining invisible while appearing intimate. The person is so focused on witnessing others that their own interior never has to be known. The Adventure persona in its authentic register creates a life of genuine expansion. In its defensive register it becomes motion as avoidance. If you are always moving, you never have to arrive anywhere that requires stillness.

Quadrant Four scores where on this spectrum the person currently sits in dating contexts. Not globally. Romantic activation changes everything. A person can be operating in the authentic register at work and in the defensive register in relationships, and they may have no awareness that the switch has occurred.

A low defense score means the persona is doing genuine work. The report affirms the persona as identity, names the growth edge clearly, and describes what fuller expression of this persona looks like in partnership.

A high defense score means the persona is load-bearing. It is holding something in place. The report names what the persona is protecting against, describes what partners are likely experiencing on the receiving end of the defensive version, and articulates what would become possible if the persona could be held more lightly. It does this without pathologizing. The defense developed for a reason, and that reason deserves acknowledgment before the cost is named.

**Axis Two: Awareness versus Automaticity**

Some people have a conscious relationship with their persona. They know they lead with Leadership or Peace or Allure. They may have language for it, have heard it from partners, have worked on it in therapy. This does not mean they can interrupt the pattern. Awareness and behavior change are different systems. But it means the report can speak to them at a different level of directness. It can name things explicitly because the person already has some relationship with being named.

Others are fully fused with their persona. It is not something they do. It is who they are. The persona feels like neutral ground, like personality, like just the way things are. This is not a failure of self-knowledge. It is what automaticity looks like. The pattern runs faster than reflection. It was established early enough that there is no memory of being different.

For automatic personas, the report works inductively. It describes the experience from the inside with enough precision that the person recognizes themselves in it before the report draws the inference. The recognition must arrive before the claim. Otherwise the claim lands as accusation rather than insight.

A high awareness score means the report can be explicit and direct. It can name the pattern, name what it protects, and name what it costs without extensive scaffolding. These people often experience the most precise reports as relief rather than confrontation.

A high automaticity score means the report works experientially first. It describes what Tuesday feels like, what happens in the body when a partner gets too close, what the person tells themselves when they pull back. It earns the right to the inference by demonstrating it already understands the experience before it names what is underneath.

**Axis Three: Dating-Specific Amplification**

The RELATE persona describes the person across contexts. Romantic activation amplifies specific Dimensions and suppresses others in ways that are often invisible to the person because the amplification feels like just how they are when they care about someone.

Every persona has a predictable amplification pattern under romantic pressure. Leadership amplifies toward control when threat activates. The person who leads effectively in low-stakes environments begins managing the relationship when they feel insecure, because decisiveness is the only tool available to a nervous system that has not learned to tolerate relational uncertainty. Stability amplifies toward rigidity. The person whose steadiness is genuinely regulating in ordinary life becomes inflexible when change threatens the structure that makes them feel safe. Peace amplifies toward conflict avoidance. The person who creates genuine calm begins disappearing from hard conversations because the cost of disruption has become higher than the cost of self-erasure. Allure amplifies toward validation dependency. The person whose magnetism is genuinely charming begins requiring the room's attention to feel okay in ways that quietly reorganize the relationship around their need to be noticed.

These amplification patterns are not character flaws. They are the persona's defensive version activated by stakes. The higher the stakes, the more pronounced the amplification. Early dating, when the person does not yet care much, often shows the authentic persona clearly. Six months in, when attachment has formed and loss has become real, is when the amplification pattern becomes visible to partners.

A low amplification score means the persona holds relatively stable across low and high stakes contexts. The report notes this as genuine strength. It takes real internal security to remain consistent when the relationship matters.

A high amplification score means the report describes the specific amplification pattern for this persona, what it looks like to the person on the inside versus what partners are experiencing on the outside, and what the gap between those two perspectives costs the relationship over time.

### How the Three Axes Combine

The three axes do not average into a single modifier. They interact specifically and the interactions are clinically meaningful.

High defense with high awareness produces a person who can see the pattern and has not yet been able to change it. This is one of the most important Profiles the report can serve because the gap between insight and behavior is a specific kind of suffering that is rarely named directly. The report for this combination names the gap explicitly and addresses why awareness alone does not resolve it.

High defense with high automaticity produces a person who is likely to experience parts of the report as surprising or mildly confronting. The report earns its way into those claims carefully, starting with the experience before the interpretation.

Low defense with high amplification produces a person who is generally integrated but loses range under high stakes. The report honors the genuine health in the low-defense score while being precise about the specific condition that collapses it.

High defense with high amplification and high automaticity is the Profile that most needs this instrument. This person's partners have likely tried to describe what they experience and been met with genuine incomprehension. The report is potentially the first time this person receives a description of their relational pattern that is specific enough to land.

### Integration with the Other Three Quadrants

Quadrant Four does not run parallel to the other three Quadrants. It integrates with them, and that integration is where the report's most important claims are generated.

Quadrant One explains how the persona's defensive register developed. A chronic caregiver wound with no repair history often produces personas that are load-bearing in exactly the way Axis One describes. The persona formed as the adaptation and has never been revised because nothing has provided sufficient corrective experience. The Quadrant One Profile tells the report which explanation of the defense to use.

Quadrant Two explains what the persona is protecting against. A Leadership persona with a shame trigger is doing different work from a Leadership persona with a fear of abandonment trigger. The shame trigger means Leadership is organized around never being found inadequate. The abandonment trigger means Leadership is organized around never being in a position where someone else's departure can hurt them. Same behavioral presentation. Completely different internal architecture. Completely different relational intervention. The Quadrant Two Profile tells the report what the defense is actually defending against.

Quadrant Three explains how the persona behaves in the critical gap between feeling and action. A Peace persona with an impulsive action decision mode is not actually peaceful. They are suppressing until they cannot, and then acting in ways that surprise everyone including themselves. A Leadership persona with a silence and withdrawal decision mode leads everywhere except the moment the relationship needs them most, which is precisely when they disappear. The Quadrant Three Profile tells the report where the persona fails under maximum load.

### Scoring Note

The questions for this Quadrant work obliquely against all three axes simultaneously. The person should not be able to identify which axis a question is probing. Questions that ask directly about self-awareness produce socially desirable answers. Questions that ask about specific past relational moments, about what partners have said, about what happens when a relationship reaches a specific kind of pressure, about the gap between who the person intends to be in relationships and who they actually find themselves being, these produce the diagnostic signal. The question bank includes several questions that approach the same underlying construct from different angles, behavioral, emotional, and relational, because consistency across angles is itself diagnostic, and tension between angles is more diagnostic still.
